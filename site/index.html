What this project is about

A phylogenetic tree shows which species are close relatives. Building one from whole genomes usually needs an alignment, which is slow and fragile. Q-MAWS skips alignment and asks honest questions about how well that works.

The problem

ML-MAWS (Saha et al., 2026) describes each genome by its minimal absent words: short words that do not occur although their parts do. It then searches all trees at once with maximum likelihood. That search is slow, and its statistical correction does not match how the data were filtered.

The idea

Keep the same data, but split the big problem into small ones. Four species have only three possible trees, so each group of four can be solved exactly. A small likelihood model, corrected for how absent-word data arise, weighs the three trees; then all groups are joined into one tree.

What we found

On new simulated data, Q-MAWS was the most accurate of the absent-word methods. Tools based on present words (Mash, Peafowl) were more accurate still. Q-MAWS is much faster for small and medium datasets but not for large ones. All of this was tested as planned and is reported openly.

Actual results

Hypotheses H1 to H5: what happened

Each tab shows the question, the result in plain words, the formal outcome as planned, and an interactive chart. Hover over a chart for exact values.

Fewer tree errors than ML-MAWS under gene transfer?

When bacteria pass genes to each other, parts of a genome tell a different story. We compared Q-MAWS with ML-MAWS on the seven datasets with known gene transfer, using two error measures: nRF counts wrong branches and nQD counts wrong four-species relationships (lower is better for both).

In plain words: on wrong branches Q-MAWS was comparable or better: lower on the four simulated datasets with transfer and equal on the other three. On four-species relationships it was worse on all seven.

Source: results/summary/h1_pairs.tsv, h1.tsv. ML-MAWS values are those reported in its paper, as planned.

Faster than ML-MAWS with many species?

Both programs ran on the same laptop, one run at a time, on simulated genomes with 25, 50 and 100 species (three of each). The rule: Q-MAWS must be faster in every run with 100 or more species.

In plain words: Q-MAWS was much faster for small and medium datasets, but its time grows with the number of four-species groups (about m⁴), while ML-MAWS works on a fixed number of columns. By 100 species ML-MAWS was faster.

Two things found on the way: ML-MAWS as released stops without a tree above 50 species with IQ-TREE 2.4.0, so its runs were completed with the IQ-TREE command of its own normal mode (Amendment 6); and 200 species would need about 12 GB of memory for Q-MAWS, so that size was left out before it ran (Amendment 7).

Source: results/scaling/h2_values.tsv. Time axis is logarithmic.

Is the likelihood safe from long-branch attraction?

If two species change fast, they look alike by chance and simple counting groups them wrongly. We simulated four-species data where two species change fast and asked how often each weighting finds the true tree: W1 (simple voting) and W2 (the conditioned likelihood).

In plain words: with more data, voting becomes surer of the wrong answer (0% at 100,000 characters), while the likelihood finds the right one (100%, 100% and 98.5%). It is the one hypothesis that was clearly supported.

Source: results/h3/recovery.csv; 200 replicates per point. Solid lines: W2; dashed lines: W1.

Are the support values trustworthy?

A support value of 0.8 should mean the branch is right about 80% of the time. On 150 branches with known truth we measured the calibration error (ECE, lower is better) of Q-MAWS's quartet support S1, its bootstrap support S2, and ML-MAWS's UFBoot.

In plain words: S1 and UFBoot are practically equal, but fail in opposite ways: S1 is too cautious at the top, UFBoot too confident. S2 has less than half their error.

Source: results/h4/calibration_*.tsv. Dot size = number of branches; dashed line = perfect calibration.

Does the "improved" version win on new data?

An exploratory analysis suggested that simple vote weights (Q-MAWS v2) might be better. Before testing it, we wrote the test down and made 20 new simulated datasets (30 species; 0, 10, 20 and 40% of genes transferred) that no part of the method had seen.

In plain words: the improvement did not replicate: v2 was not better than ML-MAWS. But the original Q-MAWS method beat ML-MAWS on 15 of the 20 datasets. This is exactly why the plan was fixed in advance.

Source: results/h5/h5_values.tsv, results/baselines/values.tsv. Median over the 5 datasets of each transfer level.

Comparisons

Q-MAWS beside other methods

Because ML-MAWS is a preprint, three peer-reviewed tools were also run on the same data with their default settings: Mash (k-mer distances), CD-MAWS (cosine distance on absent words) and Peafowl (likelihood on present k-mers). All runs finished, and the published values of Peafowl and CD-MAWS were reproduced.

Nine AFproject datasets with reference trees. Source: results/baselines/values.tsv, results/summary/qmaws.tsv.
